Crystalite: A Lightweight Transformer for
Efficient Crystal Modeling

A simpler walk through Crystalite: why we built a lighter crystal model, how the periodic table becomes part of the representation, and what the benchmarks say.

Introduction

Discovering new crystalline materials is hard for a simple reason: the space of possible compositions and structures is huge, but only a small fraction of them are actually good candidates. In principle, expensive first-principles calculations can test whether a material makes sense. In practice, that quickly becomes too slow if the goal is broad exploration.

This is why generative models are attractive. Instead of checking candidates one by one, we train a model to propose plausible crystals directly. But crystal generation is also a geometric problem. Atoms live inside a periodic lattice, distances wrap around the unit cell, and small geometric errors matter. Because of that, many strong models rely on heavy equivariant graph neural networks.

Crystalite asks whether we really need all of that machinery. Can a smaller diffusion Transformer still work if we give it the right inductive bias? The answer seems to be yes. The trick is not to ignore geometry, but to place it carefully: in the representation, in the lattice parameterization, and directly inside attention.

This post is the lighter version of the paper. I am skipping many implementation details and focusing on the core ideas: how the representation works, where the geometry enters, and what the model achieves.

Representation

Subatomic Tokenization

One of the main ideas in Crystalite is that atom identity should not be treated as a completely disconnected label. In a standard one-hot encoding, sodium is no closer to lithium than it is to xenon. Chemically, that is a poor fit. We instead use a compact chemically structured atom representation, and the interactive periodic table below is the best way to understand the intuition behind it.

Rather than showing a separate static tokenization figure, I want this embed to be the centerpiece of the explanation. The representation is built from properties that already live on the periodic table, such as period, group, block, and valence-shell information. That gives the diffusion model a smoother chemical space and makes atom prediction less brittle than recovering sparse one-hot vectors.

In the paper, atom types are ultimately handled through continuous vectors rather than one-hot identities. For the blogpost, the important part is the idea: the model does not start from “element as arbitrary ID,” but from “element as chemically structured object.”

$$ \mathcal{C} = (\mathbf{H}, \mathbf{F}, \mathbf{y}), \qquad \mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d_H}, \quad \mathbf{F} \in [0,1)^{N \times 3}, \quad \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{6}. $$

Here \( \mathbf{H} \) is the continuous atom-type channel, \( \mathbf{F} \) contains the fractional coordinates, and \( \mathbf{y} \) is a compact latent description of the lattice. Those three parts are denoised jointly.

Method

A compact diffusion transformer with geometric attention

The backbone of Crystalite is intentionally simple. Each atom becomes a token, and the lattice becomes one additional global token. The model then processes this sequence with a standard Transformer trunk conditioned on the diffusion noise level.

$$ \mathbf{t}_i^{\mathrm{atom}} = E_H(\mathbf{h}_i) + E_F(\mathbf{f}_i), \qquad \mathbf{t}^{\mathrm{lat}} = E_{\mathrm{lat}}(\mathbf{y}). $$

This already gives the network a clean decomposition: one token per atom, plus one token for the global cell geometry. To keep the lattice stable during diffusion, the paper uses a lower-triangular latent parameterization:

$$ \mathbf{L}(\mathbf{y}) = \begin{bmatrix} e^{y_1} & 0 & 0 \\ y_2 & e^{y_3} & 0 \\ y_4 & y_5 & e^{y_6} \end{bmatrix}. $$

This is a small design choice, but it matters. It keeps the parameterization unconstrained while ensuring the diagonal terms stay positive.

The main extra ingredient is the Geometric Enhancement Module (GEM). Rather than replacing the Transformer with a much heavier geometry-specific backbone, GEM adds periodic pairwise geometric information directly to the attention logits.

$$ A_{\mathrm{GEM}} = \frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}} + B_{\mathrm{geom}}. $$

In plain language: the model still uses ordinary self-attention, but the scores are nudged using crystal geometry under periodic boundary conditions. So the backbone stays simple, while the attention becomes much more crystal-aware.

Training is then just diffusion over the three channels, with a weighted loss that combines atom-type, coordinate, and lattice denoising:

$$ \mathcal{L} = \lambda_H \mathcal{L}_H + \lambda_F \mathcal{L}_F + \lambda_{\mathrm{lat}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{lat}}. $$
$$ \mathcal{L}_F = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_F(\sigma) \left\| \mathrm{wrap}\!\left(\hat{\mathbf{f}}_i - \mathbf{f}_i\right) \right\|_2^2. $$

The coordinate term uses wrapped residuals in fractional space, which is exactly what you want for periodic data.

Results

State-of-the-art CSP and strong de novo generation

We evaluate Crystalite in two settings. In crystal structure prediction (CSP), the composition is known and the model predicts the structure. In de novo generation, the model generates atom types, coordinates, and lattice from scratch.

The CSP story is very clean: Crystalite is best across all three benchmarks in both match rate and RMSE. What I find especially striking is the RMSE. The model is not only recovering the right structure more often, it is recovering the geometry more accurately.

$$ \begin{array}{lcccccc} \textbf{Model} & \textbf{MP-20 MR} \uparrow & \textbf{MP-20 RMSE} \downarrow & \textbf{MPTS-52 MR} \uparrow & \textbf{MPTS-52 RMSE} \downarrow & \textbf{Alex-MP-20 MR} \uparrow & \textbf{Alex-MP-20 RMSE} \downarrow \\ \hline \text{CDVAE} & 33.90 & 0.1045 & 5.34 & 0.2106 & -- & -- \\ \text{DiffCSP} & 51.49 & 0.0631 & 12.19 & 0.1786 & -- & -- \\ \text{FlowMM} & 61.39 & 0.0566 & 17.54 & 0.1726 & -- & -- \\ \text{CrystalFlow}& 62.02 & 0.0710 & 22.71 & 0.1548 & -- & -- \\ \text{KLDM} & 65.83 & 0.0517 & 23.93 & 0.1276 & -- & -- \\ \text{OMatG} & 63.75 & 0.0720 & 25.15 & 0.1931 & 64.71 & 0.1251 \\ \mathbf{Crystalite} & \mathbf{66.05} & \mathbf{0.0329} & \mathbf{31.49} & \mathbf{0.0701} & \mathbf{67.52} & \mathbf{0.0335} \end{array} $$

In our experiments, GEM has only a small effect on whether the model lands in the correct structural mode, but it consistently improves geometric refinement. In practice, that shows up as a roughly twenty percent reduction in RMSE across experiments.

For de novo generation, the picture is more nuanced. There is no single metric that settles everything, because validity, novelty, uniqueness, and stability pull in different directions. Crystalite’s main strength is that it hits a very strong compromise point instead of over-optimizing one axis at the expense of the others.

$$ \begin{array}{lcccccccccc} \textbf{Model} & \textbf{Struct.\ Val.} \uparrow & \textbf{Comp.\ Val.} \uparrow & \textbf{Unique} \uparrow & \textbf{Novel} \uparrow & \textbf{U.N.} \uparrow & \textbf{Stable} \uparrow & \textbf{S.U.N.} \uparrow & \textbf{wdist-}\rho \downarrow & \textbf{wdist N-ary} \downarrow & \textbf{Time/1k} \downarrow \\ \hline \text{FlowMM} & 93.03 & 83.15 & 97.44 & 85.00 & 83.99 & 46.05 & 31.64 & 1.389 & \mathbf{0.075} & -- \\ \text{CrystalDiT} & 77.82 & 67.28 & 90.88 & 59.33 & 56.86 & \mathbf{83.41} & 41.70 & 0.202 & 0.171 & -- \\ \text{DiffCSP} & \mathbf{99.93} & 82.10 & 96.90 & 89.53 & 87.89 & 50.28 & 38.60 & 0.192 & 0.344 & -- \\ \text{MatterGen} & 99.78 & 83.72 & \mathbf{98.10} & \mathbf{91.14} & \mathbf{90.26} & 51.70 & 42.29 & 0.088 & 0.184 & -- \\ \text{ADiT} & 99.52 & \mathbf{90.15} & 90.25 & 59.80 & 56.91 & 76.90 & 36.76 & 0.231 & 0.089 & -- \\ \mathbf{Crystalite} & 99.61 & 81.94 & 95.33 & 79.15 & 77.12 & 70.97 & \mathbf{48.55} & \mathbf{0.046} & 0.125 & -- \\ \end{array} $$

The number I would emphasize most is the S.U.N. score. It combines three things that matter for actual discovery: the material should be stable, unique, and novel. Crystalite gets the best value there, while also achieving the best density distribution match in the comparison.

This matters because some generation metrics are really properties of the whole sampled set, not of one isolated sample. Uniqueness is the clearest example. If you generate more and more crystals, duplicates will eventually accumulate. So a strong generator is not just one that looks good at 10,000 samples, but one that continues to discover diverse candidates as the sampling budget grows.

Discussion

What a lighter model buys us

The most interesting part of these results, to me, is not that geometry has become unimportant. It has not. The interesting part is that we do not always need to encode geometry through a very heavy architecture. A Transformer can be competitive if the geometric information enters in the right places.

That has practical value. Lighter models are easier to train, easier to sample from, and easier to plug into larger screening workflows. They are also easier to extend. If you want stronger conditioning, larger-scale pretraining, or a tighter inverse-design loop, it helps to start from a backbone that is compact rather than overloaded.

The paper also makes a more general point about evaluation. In crystal generation, there is no honest “best” model without saying which trade-off you care about. A model can improve stability by becoming more conservative, or improve novelty by becoming less reliable. Crystalite looks strongest when the target is balanced discovery performance rather than one isolated metric.

Conclusion

Simple is not the same as naive

Crystalite is a lightweight diffusion Transformer for crystal modeling, but the word lightweight here should not be confused with generic. The whole point is that the model stays small while being carefully shaped around the structure of the problem: chemistry enters through a structured atom representation, the lattice gets its own compact parameterization, and periodic geometry is injected directly into attention.

That combination is enough to produce state-of-the-art results on crystal structure prediction and very strong de novo generation performance. The broader lesson is that there is useful space between two extremes: fully generic Transformers on one side, and very heavy equivariant message-passing models on the other. Crystalite sits in that middle ground.

For materials discovery, that middle ground looks promising: faster models, cleaner architectures, and still enough geometric awareness to be genuinely useful.
